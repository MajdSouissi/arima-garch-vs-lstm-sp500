--- Statistical vs Practical Significance ---
  With N = 6,537, even tiny autocorrelations become statistically
  significant. The 95% confidence bound is ±0.02424.

  Key question: Are these autocorrelations economically meaningful?

  The largest ACF values are on the order of 0.03–0.06.
  This means at most ~0.3% of return variance at any lag is explained
  by the previous return. The R² of an AR(1) model would be ≈ ρ₁² ≈ 0.010390.

  INTERPRETATION:
  • Statistically: Ljung-Box rejects the null at all tested lags.
    There IS detectable linear dependence.
  • Practically: The magnitudes are very small. This weak dependence
    is consistent with microstructure effects (bid-ask bounce,
    non-synchronous trading) rather than exploitable predictability.
  • Modeling implication: A low-order ARMA(p,q) with p,q ∈ {0,1}
    may modestly improve the conditional mean, but the primary value
    of modeling the mean is to obtain cleaner residuals for GARCH.

--- Model Selection Rationale ---
  1. GARCH(1,1): The workhorse volatility model. Parsimonious and
     empirically adequate for most financial series. This is the
     baseline that any alternative must beat.

  2. GJR-GARCH(1,1): Captures asymmetric volatility response.
     Motivated by the sign-bias test and leverage effect evidence.
     γ > 0 means negative shocks increase volatility more than
     positive shocks of equal magnitude.

  3. EGARCH(1,1): Alternative asymmetric specification.
     Advantages: no non-negativity constraints on parameters;
     models log(σ²) so σ² is automatically positive.

  Innovation distributions to test:
    - Normal (Gaussian)
    - Student-t (heavier tails)
  Motivated by excess kurtosis ≈ 11 and Jarque-Bera rejection.

1. IS A CONDITIONAL MEAN MODEL NECESSARY?
   Not strictly necessary. The autocorrelations in log returns are
   statistically significant (Ljung-Box rejects at all lags) but
   economically small in magnitude (|ρ| < 0.06 at all lags).
   The R² of an AR(1) is approximately 0.01039.
   A constant-mean model is defensible as a baseline.
   A low-order ARMA may modestly improve residuals for GARCH.

2. WHAT LOW-ORDER ARMA STRUCTURE IS DEFENSIBLE?
   Selected model: MA(1)
   AIC = 20958.30, BIC = 20978.65
   The improvement over constant-mean is modest.
   Both ARMA(0,0) and MA(1) are defensible.
   For the GARCH stage, we test both.

3. IS THERE REMAINING ARCH BEHAVIOR AFTER MODELING THE MEAN?
   YES. ARCH-LM test is highly significant (p ≈ 0) for ALL
   mean specifications tested. ARCH effects are NOT an artifact
   of mean misspecification.

4. IS GARCH(1,1) A REASONABLE BASELINE?
   YES. GARCH(1,1) is the standard benchmark for financial
   volatility. Our data show high persistence (α+β close to 1)
   and significant ARCH effects — exactly the conditions where
   GARCH(1,1) is known to perform well.

5. IS THERE EVIDENCE SUGGESTING ASYMMETRIC VOLATILITY?
   YES.
   Sign bias test confirms asymmetric response.
   Negative returns are followed by higher volatility (leverage effect).
   GJR-GARCH and EGARCH specifications are empirically motivated.

6. IS STUDENT-t INNOVATION DISTRIBUTION WORTH TESTING?
   YES. Even after GARCH filtering, standardized residuals show
   excess kurtosis, indicating that GARCH alone does not fully
   explain the heavy tails. Student-t innovations consistently
   improve AIC/BIC across all volatility specifications.

7. RECOMMENDED SPECIFICATIONS FOR OUT-OF-SAMPLE EVALUATION:
   Best by BIC: const+GJR-GARCH(1,1)+t (BIC=17353.27)
   Best by AIC: const+GJR-GARCH(1,1)+t (AIC=17312.56)
